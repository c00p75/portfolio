## Why an automated market maker

A prediction market with no liquidity isn't a market. It's a form. An order book
needs traders on both sides before it can quote anything, and a platform launching
new markets continuously never has that on day one.

A scoring-rule market maker always quotes, and its prices move as a function of
the positions held rather than of who happens to be online. The cost is that the
platform itself carries inventory risk, bounded by the liquidity parameter. That's
a trade you make on purpose, in exchange for a market that works from the very
first trade.

## Why the ledger is double-entry

Because the alternative is a balance column, and a balance column can't tell you
when it's wrong. In a system taking deposits, charging fees and paying out
settlements, the property you want isn't that the arithmetic is correct. It's that
incorrect arithmetic is *detectable*. Double-entry hands you an invariant to check
instead of a number to trust.

## Reconciliation: why exceptions rather than validation

The instinct with financial data is to validate on the way in and reject whatever
doesn't balance. That instinct is wrong here, and understanding why is most of the
design.

A finance office adopting a reconciliation tool already has inconsistent records.
That's why they're adopting it. A system that refuses the inconsistent ones
imports a clean subset and leaves the actual problem sitting outside the tool. So
every check writes exceptions instead: typed, tracked, assignable and countable.
The product is the worklist.

## Reconciliation: the part that is genuinely hard

The five checks aren't independent, and they can contradict each other. A record
can sit inside budget, tie out against revenue, and still fail the general-ledger
match. Which of those an administrator should act on depends on context the system
doesn't have. Most checks recompute on every write so the position stays current.
The general-ledger tie-out is heavy enough that it runs on demand and after a
journal import instead.

Deciding which checks are cheap enough to run continuously and which have to be
explicit is the same shape of problem as splitting risk limits between an order
path and a supervision loop, elsewhere in this portfolio. The cost of evaluating a
rule determines where that rule is allowed to live.
